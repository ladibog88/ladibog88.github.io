LogBees

Travel Logging Bot + Live Scrapbook — South America 2026

A Telegram bot built for a 42-day journey through South America with Min. Send a photo, a Google Maps link, or a voice reflection — the bot captures it, stores it in Google Sheets, uploads media to Cloudinary, and publishes everything to a live scrapbook with an interactive map. Two sites: one public, one for family with a password gate.

Python Telegram Bot API Google Sheets Cloudinary Leaflet.js Railway Cloudflare Pages

The Story

Min and I had a 42-day trip through South America lined up — Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina. We both wanted to document it properly. Not just Instagram posts after the fact, but a real-time record: where we were, what we ate, what we thought, what surprised us.

I built LogBees before we left. The idea was simple: a Telegram bot that required zero friction to use. Take a photo — send it. Drop a Google Maps link from the restaurant you just loved — send it. Type a thought before you sleep — send it. Everything gets captured, timestamped, and geolocated automatically.

Two different audiences needed two different sites. The public TravelBees scrapbook is open to anyone. The family site is password-gated and shows a bit more — it's anonymous (no names) but lets family feel close to the journey. The hard deadline of May 31st departure focused every design decision perfectly.

How the Bot Works

📸

Send a photo

Bot uploads it to Cloudinary, stores the URL and caption in Google Sheets with timestamp and location

📍

Send a Google Maps link

Bot parses the URL (many formats — short links, place names, coords), reverse geocodes it, and extracts a clean place name + coordinates for the map

✍️

Send a reflection

Any plain text message gets logged as a reflection entry — timestamped and ready to browse in the scrapbook

🔄

/undo

Instantly removes the last entry — for when you send something to the wrong chat or change your mind

📋

/summary

Generates a quick stats rundown: total entries, photos, places visited, days logged

💭

/reflect

Sends a daily prompt — a gentle nudge to write something reflective before sleeping

Two Sites, Two Audiences

TravelBees (Public)

Open to anyone with the link. Leaflet.js interactive map with all visited locations, photo gallery, and reflections. Compact card view with expandable details.

Family Site (Private)

Password-gated (password set before departure). Anonymous — no names used, just "we." Shows more detail than the public site. Feels close to the journey without being a public blog.

Interactive Map

Every Google Maps link becomes a pin on a Leaflet.js map. Click a pin to see what happened there — photos, notes, timestamp. The journey unfolds geographically.

Live Updates

The site updates in real-time as entries come in. Family watching at home see new photos appear as we send them — no refresh needed, no waiting for a blog post.

What I Learned

🗺

Google Maps URLs Are a Jungle

There are at least six different Google Maps URL formats in the wild — short links, place names, coordinates, embedded maps, search results. I had to handle them all. Reading the actual URL spec carefully saved hours of debugging on the road.

📅

Hard Deadlines Are a Gift

The trip started May 31st. That date was non-negotiable. It forced me to scope ruthlessly — build the logging core first, ship by departure, iterate during the trip. The hardest design constraint was also the most clarifying.

👥

Two Audiences, Two Designs

Public and family are different audiences with different needs. The public site is curated and polished. The family site is more raw and personal. Designing for two audiences simultaneously — and keeping them coherent — was harder than designing either one in isolation.

📍

Reverse Geocoding Is Surprisingly Tricky

Getting a human-readable "Café Tortoni, Buenos Aires" from a set of coordinates — especially in countries with inconsistent address data — required building fallbacks for when the API returns nothing useful.

🚀

Ship Before You're Ready

I used the bot from day one of the trip, bugs and all. That real usage on the road surfaced issues no amount of local testing would have caught — and forced me to fix them quickly. Shipping early is always better than shipping perfect.
